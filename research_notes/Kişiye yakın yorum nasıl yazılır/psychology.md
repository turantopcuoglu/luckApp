# Psychology of "That's So Me": Why People Accept (and Reject) Personality and Horoscope Readings

Scope note: ~20 searches and fetches. Several primary papers (Dickson & Kelly 1985 PDF is a scanned image; Furnham & Schofield 1987; Snyder et al. 1977) could only be read through abstracts and secondary summaries. Hyman's 1977 article was read in full on skepdic.com and is the richest primary source here. Where a number comes only from a secondary source, that is stated.

## 1. Barnum/Forer effect: original study, reviews and moderators of acceptance

### Takeaway
People rate a single generic sketch as a very accurate description of themselves (about 4.3 out of 5 in Forer 1949). Acceptance goes up when the text is framed as "prepared for you", rests on personal data such as a full birth date, is mostly favourable, comes from a trusted or prestigious source, and the reader already believes. Even skeptics rate text labelled as astrological higher than the same text without the label.

### Cited Findings
**Forer (1949), the original study**
- Forer, B. R. (1949). "The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility." *J. Abnormal and Social Psychology*, 44(1), 118–123. 39 psychology students took a test and each got the "same" sketch, which Forer had taken from a newsstand astrology book. — [Wikipedia: Barnum effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- The reported mean accuracy differs by source: 4.26/5 in Hyman's account, 4.30 or 4.31 elsewhere. Hyman adds that 16 of 39 (about 41%) rated it a perfect fit and only 5 rated it below 4. Students in the 1970s gave nearly the same rating. — [Hyman 1977, via Skepdic](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Paul Meehl named it the "Barnum effect" in "Wanted – A Good Cookbook" (*American Psychologist*, 1956). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Content of the Forer sketch, paraphrased by Hyman. Each item is a reusable pattern:
  - unrealistic aspirations
  - swings between sociable and withdrawn
  - an independent thinker
  - wants variety but feels restricted
  - doubts past decisions
  - looks controlled outside but feels insecure inside
  - unused potential
  - self-critical
  - needs others' approval

  — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)

**Reviews**
- Dickson, D. H., & Kelly, I. W. (1985). "The 'Barnum effect' in personality assessment: A review of the literature." *Psychological Reports*, 57, 367–382. It concludes that acceptance "depends on the relevance and favorability of the profile and to some extent on the type of assessment utilized." It reviews generality and supposed relevance, favourability, type of assessment procedure, and origin and format of the interpretation. — [abstract via search / crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2466%2Fpr0.1985.57.2.367); [PDF (scanned)](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)
- Some secondary commentary says this review treats favourability as a covariate of the profile's real accuracy rather than a direct cause of acceptance. This could not be verified from the full text. — [search summary of review](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)
- Furnham, A., & Schofield, S. (1987). "Accepting personality test feedback: A review of the Barnum effect." *Current Psychological Research & Reviews*, 6(2), 162–178. Later papers call it "the most comprehensive review." It traces the effect to two causes: the test-taker's suggestibility or the test's inadequacy. The full text was not accessed. — [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/e6a7c0ac7b59cbcad7304415264bf8d1); [citing paper (Poškus, VU)](https://www.zurnalai.vu.lt/psichologija/article/download/4893/3148/)

**Moderator: perceived personalization ("for you")**
- Snyder & Larson (1972) were the first to test whether labelling a general interpretation "for you" raises acceptance. Replications followed: Snyder 1974; Snyder, Larsen & Bloom 1976; Snyder & Shenkel 1975, 1976; Jackson 1978; Baillargeon & Danis 1984. — [search summary citing these](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)
- Snyder's group (Snyder & Shenkel 1975, as reported by Hyman): the same sketch labelled "for you, personally" averaged about 4.5. Labelled as "generally true of people", it averaged about 3–4. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Snyder, Larsen & Bloom (1976): people told the interpretation came from an assessment procedure accepted it more than people told it was "generally true of people." — [search summary](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)
- Counter-finding: one 1974 study by Snyder and colleagues found that neither test type nor the "for you" vs "people in general" framing changed acceptance. The effect is not uniform. — [search summary](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)

**Moderator: personal data such as birth date (most relevant to this app)**
- In Snyder's astrology condition, the same generic horoscope got higher ratings the more birth data people gave:

  | Birth data given | Mean accuracy (of 5) |
  |---|---|
  | None | ≈3.2 |
  | Year and month | 3.76 |
  | Year, month and day | 4.98 |

  — [Hyman 1977, reporting Snyder & Shenkel](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)

**Moderator: favourability**
- Favourable profiles are accepted more readily than unfavourable ones (Dickson & Kelly 1985). The ratio of positive to negative statements matters (Claridge et al. 2008, *Personality and Individual Differences* 44(2)). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Hyman's own 1950s item studies found the most convincing mix was about 75% desirable, specific-seeming items and 25% undesirable, general-seeming items. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- One small study (N=27) found that self-serving bias can cancel the Barnum effect. The authors note the effect usually appears with positive traits. — [SBP Journal](https://aws.sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/1190)

**Moderator: authority, prestige and trust**
- Acceptance is higher when people believe in the evaluator's authority (Dickson & Kelly 1985) and trust the provider's honesty (Claridge et al. 2008). High-status sources make negative statements more acceptable (Furnham 2014, *Psychology Today*). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Asch (1948): the same passage was approved when attributed to Jefferson and rejected when attributed to Lenin, because the source changes how the words are read. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Hyman's 1950s finding: a sketch labelled "astrology" was rated lower overall than the same sketch labelled "a new personality test," yet the individual statements were rated equally true. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)

**Moderator: vagueness and qualifiers**
- Vague statements, especially those hedged with "at times," are accepted more (Krauss-Whitbourne 2010, *Psychology Today*, secondary). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)

**Moderator: sheep/goat (believer vs skeptic)**
- Glick, Gottesman & Jolton (1989). "The fault is not in the stars: Susceptibility of skeptics and believers in astrology to the Barnum effect." *PSPB*, 15, 572–583.
  - Believers accepted generic descriptions more than skeptics did.
  - Skeptics as well as believers rated astrological descriptions as more accurate than identical non-astrological ones.
  - Skeptics who got favourable horoscopes became significantly more positive toward astrology. No other group shifted.

  — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167289154010)

**Moderator: individual differences and sex**
- People with authoritarian or neurotic traits, or a high need for approval, show more of the effect (Furnham 2014, secondary). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Sundberg (1955): both sexes rated stock sketches favourably, and women responded more warmly. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Stagner (1958), "The Gullibility of Personnel Managers": more than half of personnel managers rated generic feedback accurate, and almost none rated it wrong. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)

### Inferences
- For an app that already collects birth date (and possibly name and time), the Snyder birth-data gradient is the strongest single lever. Visibly using the data ("for someone born on 14 March, with the Moon in X today…") should raise perceived accuracy even when the core text is semi-generic.
- Glick et al. suggest favourable readings work on skeptics too and can soften their stance. A positive overall tone is not only for believers.
- Framing matters as much as content. Hyman found the same statements rated equally true but the whole sketch rated lower when labelled astrology. The app's framing ("based on your chart and today's sky", plus visible calculation) acts as the "prestige of the assessment procedure."

### Gaps
- Exact Forer mean (4.26 vs 4.30/4.31) is unresolved without the original paper.
- Dickson & Kelly (1985) and Furnham & Schofield (1987) full texts were not readable. Effect sizes per moderator are not available.
- The Snyder birth-data numbers come from Hyman's secondary report. The original is likely Snyder (1974), "Why horoscopes are true," *J. Clinical Psychology*. Not verified.

## 2. Snyder et al. on self-description acceptance; Glick et al.; specificity vs vagueness; which negative statements are accepted

### Takeaway
Acceptance rises with "for you" framing, perceived assessment effort and personal data. Specificity cuts both ways. Statements that seem specific but are true of almost everyone are best. Statements that are actually specific are accepted only when they happen to fit. Negative statements are accepted when they are general, a minority of the text, framed as a hidden or inner trait, and wrapped in favourable context.

### Cited Findings
- Snyder, Shenkel & Lowery (1977). "Acceptance of personality interpretations: The 'Barnum effect' and beyond." *J. Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 45, 104–114. This is the main review of situational determinants: "for you" labelling, perceived procedure, and favourability. The specific numbers were not retrieved. — [bibliographic record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_83370882)
- Glick et al. (1989): see section 1. Belief raises acceptance, the astrology label raises it even for skeptics, and favourable horoscopes move skeptics. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167289154010)
- Specificity trade-off, Hyman's item research. The best mix was about 75% "desirable but specific-seeming" items and 25% "undesirable but general-seeming" items. So:
  - positive items should *sound* specific
  - negative items should *stay* general

  — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Which negative statements work, from the Forer sketch's negatives (paraphrased): the "control outside / insecure inside" contrast, "self-critical", "doubts decisions", "unused potential", "needs approval". All are mild, near-universal, framed as inner experience rather than visible behaviour, and many carry an implicit compliment, as in "unused potential." — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- High-status sources make negative assessments more acceptable (Furnham 2014, secondary). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Hyman's rule 12: flatter often, and pre-empt objections to flattery. A small dose of unflattering content raises believability. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Vagueness plus a frequency hedge ("at times," "sometimes") increases acceptance. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)

### Inferences
- Write negatives as *occasional inner tendencies* ("now and then you second-guess a decision you already made well"), not as stable visible flaws ("you are lazy").
- Positives can be phrased with concrete-sounding detail ("you notice small changes in people's mood before they say anything"). That detail still has a high base rate.

### Gaps
- Primary data on *which* negative statements are rejected (for example a negativity threshold, or which trait domains) was not found in accessible sources.
- No accessible study isolated "specific-seeming but high base rate" from "truly specific" items with numbers beyond Hyman's report.

## 3. Cold reading techniques (Hyman 1977; Rowland, *The Full Facts Book of Cold Reading*)

### Takeaway
Cold readers do not rely on any single trick. They pair Barnum-style statements with framing that hands the work of interpretation to the client. Contradiction-covering statements, flattery, statements about life stage, and "things will get better" predictions each exploit a different gap in how people check claims about themselves.

### Cited Findings
**Hyman (1977), "Cold reading: How to convince strangers that you know all about them"**
- First published in *The Zetetic* (now *Skeptical Inquirer*), Vol. 1, No. 2, 1977. — [search/skepdic](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Hyman's 13 rules, paraphrased:
  1. Confidence is the key ingredient.
  2. Use statistics and surveys to predict beliefs from demographics.
  3. Stay modest and avoid grand claims.
  4. Get the client's cooperation in advance, so they reinterpret vague statements and take the blame for misses.
  5. Use a gimmick (palm, tarot) for novelty and thinking time.
  6. Keep stock phrases ready.
  7. Observe.
  8. "Fish": phrase statements as questions, then restate confirmed answers as assertions.
  9. Listen.
  10. Dramatize with word pictures.
  11. Appear to know more than you say.
  12. Flatter, and pre-empt objections to flattery.
  13. Tell the client what they want to hear.

  — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Hyman's reversed-palmistry anecdote: at his mentor Stanley Saks' suggestion, he read palms deliberately opposite to the lines, and the readings were still accepted. Content mattered less than the context of the reading. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)

**Rowland, *The Full Facts Book of Cold Reading***
- Rowland's book covers about 38 techniques in four groups: character, facts and events, the future, and ways of quietly getting information. — [search summary, Rowland material](https://www.ianrowland.com/faqs/cold-reading-techniques)
- **Rainbow Ruse.** Credits the person with a trait *and its opposite*, using very common traits such as reliability and tidiness. Example (paraphrased): "you're dependable and punctual, yet there are times you end up rushed and late." It works because nearly everyone can relate to both halves. — [ianrowland.com](https://www.ianrowland.com/cold-reading-techniques)
- **Fuzzy Fact.** The reader supplies the words and the client supplies the meaning. Example (paraphrased): "an older relative, an 'M' connection" can land as Michael, and a near miss is often still accepted. — [ianrowland.com](https://www.ianrowland.com/cold-reading-techniques)
- **Pollyanna Pearl.** A difficult area of life will start to improve ("things can only get better"). Example (paraphrased): new work opportunities in about three months for someone unhappy at work. — [ianrowland.com](https://www.ianrowland.com/cold-reading-techniques)
- **Time Revision** (one of 7 "revisions"). When a claim misses, the reader moves it into the future: "maybe not yet, but soon." — [ianrowland.com](https://www.ianrowland.com/cold-reading-techniques)
- **Jacques Statement.** Sources conflict on the definition:
  - One Rowland-derived copy defines it as a statement so general anyone agrees.
  - Another ties the name to Jaques' "seven ages of man" speech in *As You Like It*, making it a statement about the person's life phase (for example, reassessment around mid-life).

  — [search summary of Rowland material](https://www.ianrowland.com/faqs/cold-reading-techniques); [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/435267490/Cold-Reading-Master-Class-pdf)
- **Sugar Lump.** A statement that gives the client a pleasant emotional reward for going along with the reading. Only one weak source (a Scribd copy) was found. — [Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/435267490/Cold-Reading-Master-Class-pdf)
- **Other technique taxonomies (SMU course notes, built on Rowland's categories):**
  - **Barnum statements**
  - **Both sides of the story:** shy yet sometimes confident, so it can't be falsified. This is the "pushy/shy" contradiction.
  - **Rewards:** praising honesty or hard work.
  - **Special thing/event:** a near-certain fact, such as an old broken gadget in the house.
  - **Not-so-WAG:** likely guesses based on age and demographics.
  - **Seasonal guess:** tied to financial, religious or sports calendars.
  - **Predictions:**
    - things will get better
    - even-chance predictions, where misses are forgotten
    - unverifiable predictions
    - predictions that can be verified only if true

  — [SMU Physics 3333: Cold Reading methods](https://www.physics.smu.edu/pseudo/ColdReading/methods.html)
- **"You vs how others see you" / inner-outer contrast.** Forer's sketch has "appears disciplined and controlled outside, but worrisome and insecure inside." This is the canonical Barnum pattern of a hidden self that others don't see. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- **Why clients accept (Rowland):**
  - Clients already believe, so they look for meaning.
  - They are polite and rarely flatly reject a statement.
  - They remember the accurate-seeming parts and forget the rest.
  - Almost nobody is fully satisfied, so "things will improve" lands.

  — [ianrowland.com](https://www.ianrowland.com/cold-reading-techniques)
- Dutton (1988), "The cold reading technique," *Experientia* 44(4), 326–332, is a peer-reviewed overview. Only the citation was retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01961271)

### Inferences
- Several techniques depend on live feedback: fishing, Time Revision, "a miss is a hit." They do not carry over to a one-way deterministic text. What does carry over:
  - Rainbow Ruse and "both sides"
  - Fuzzy Facts, through open slots the reader fills in ("someone whose name you've been thinking about")
  - Pollyanna Pearl
  - seasonal and calendar hooks
  - life-stage (Jacques) statements, keyed to age from the birth date
  - inner-vs-outer statements
  - the Sugar Lump and flattery
- Rainbow Ruse is the "inner contradiction" pattern, and it has to be signalled as nuance ("you can be both… depending on…"). Otherwise it reads as a contradiction (see section 6).

### Gaps
- No primary access to Rowland's book. The definitions of Fine Flattery, Psychic Credit, Greener Grass, Jacques Statement and Sugar Lump could not be verified. Only Rainbow Ruse, Fuzzy Fact, Pollyanna Pearl and Time Revision are confirmed from Rowland's own site.
- No empirical study measuring the acceptance gain of individual cold-reading techniques was found.

## 4. Self-verification theory (Swann)

### Takeaway
People want feedback that confirms what they already believe about themselves, including negative self-views. Feedback that clashes with a firmly held self-view is seen as less credible. The best predictor of acceptance is therefore fit with the user's self-concept, which an app can learn far better by asking than by inferring.

### Cited Findings
- People with positive self-views prefer favourable partners, while people with negative self-views prefer unfavourable ones, against the prediction of self-enhancement. More than a dozen replications in different labs support this. — [Swann lab review PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/sw_int_studies05.pdf); [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/self-verification-theory.html)
- The same people sought favourable feedback on their positive self-conceptions and unfavourable feedback on their negative ones, whatever their global self-esteem. Self-verification works *per attribute*. — [Swann lab](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/sw_int_studies05.pdf)
- Depression study: 82% of depressed participants chose unfavourable feedback, compared with 64% of low-self-esteem and 25% of high-self-esteem participants. — [Swann lab PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/sw_pel_krull.pdf)
- This is not masochism. People with negative self-views feel torn and ambivalent about unfavourable feedback. — [Swann lab](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/sw_pel_krull.pdf)
- Critique: relative to self-enhancement, self-verification is rare and limited to people with strongly negative self-views. — [summary of critiques](https://www.simplypsychology.org/self-verification-theory.html)
- A 2021 *Psychological Science* paper argues that people prefer self-verifying feedback because they find it *more accurate*. Credibility, not a motive to self-verify, often drives the effect. — [SAGE 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976211049439)

### Inferences
- Combining self-enhancement (favourability) with self-verification (fit) gives this rule: be positive *on dimensions the user already sees as strengths*, and be gently honest *on dimensions they already admit as weaknesses*.
- A short onboarding self-description (for example, choosing 3 of 10 traits: "I'm calm / restless / social / private…") would let deterministic text verify the user's self-image instead of guessing. This also fits the 2021 credibility account: matching text feels more accurate.

### Gaps
- No study found that combines self-verification with horoscope or Barnum acceptance directly.

## 5. Subjective validation, confirmation bias and selective memory

### Takeaway
Readers do the interpretive work themselves. They take vague statements and fill them from their own memories, which creates an "illusion of uniqueness." They then remember the hits and forget the misses.

### Cited Findings
- Hyman: clients actively make sense of vague statements from their own memories and expectations, like reading a poem. He calls the feeling "personal validation" and the belief an "illusion of uniqueness." He argues this is normal comprehension, not irrationality. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Hyman's "charitable/generous" priming study: context set by an earlier impression changed how later trait words were interpreted. Earlier context colours what follows. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Garfinkel: people given *random* yes/no answers by a supposed counsellor still felt understood and helped. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Weizenbaum's ELIZA ("Doctor"): a keyword program made users feel understood, and some insisted a human was typing. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- People remember hits and forget misses. Readers move on quickly after a miss. — [Randi, Skeptic.com](https://www.skeptic.com/article/how-to-convince-people-you-are-psychic/); [ianrowland.com](https://www.ianrowland.com/cold-reading-techniques)
- Even-chance and "things will get better" predictions work because misses are forgotten. — [SMU](https://www.physics.smu.edu/pseudo/ColdReading/methods.html)

### Inferences
- Leave room for projection. A statement with one concrete-sounding but open slot ("a conversation you've been putting off") gets filled with the user's own content. This is the Fuzzy Fact applied to text.
- Opening context primes how the rest is read (Hyman's priming finding). A warm, accurate-feeling first line raises acceptance of everything after it. The headline sentence matters most.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on how the order of statements (primacy) affects overall acceptance in horoscope text specifically.

## 6. Why people reject readings ("not me")

### Takeaway
Acceptance is not unconditional. When people compare matched and mismatched feedback, they can tell genuine from inverted or trivial feedback. Rejection is driven by:
- clashes with the user's self-concept
- framing the text as generic, for everyone
- missing personal data
- a less credible framing label
- obvious triviality

Real accuracy adds to acceptance on top of the Barnum effect.

### Cited Findings
- Students could reliably tell *actual* feedback from *trivial* and *inverted* feedback on uniqueness, usefulness, accuracy, and as a source of new self-knowledge. Inverted (contradicting) and trivial feedback are both penalised. — [J. Personality Assessment abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15327752jpa4305_13)
- Test sophistication and initial confidence in the procedure predicted accuracy and uniqueness ratings. — [JPA abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15327752jpa4403_14)
- "Inventory responding" models individual differences in acceptance well. Overall acceptance levels are pushed up further by persuasion. Acceptance is therefore not evidence of validity. — [JPA abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15327752jpa4802_13)
- **Big Five feedback** (Poškus et al. 2014, "Your NEO Summary"): statements were rated more accurate when they matched participants' actual Big Five scores. Validity adds acceptance on top of the Barnum effect. — [Psichologija, VU](https://www.journals.vu.lt/psichologija/article/view/3694)
- Wyman & Vyse (2008), "Science versus the Stars," *J. General Psychology*: 52 students picked their real profile from real and bogus versions. Two kinds of profile were used: NEO-FFI profiles and computer astrological natal charts. Per secondary reports, people found their real NEO profile above chance but did not find their real natal chart. This updates Carlson (1985, *Nature*). Exact figures were not retrieved. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18649494/); [Discover](https://discovermagazine.com/mind/psychology-vs-astrology)
- **When fake beats real:** in Sundberg's (1955) MMPI study, 26 of 44 students (59%) chose a generic fake sketch over one written for them from their actual results. A "true but unflattering or technical" description can lose to a flattering generic one. — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Rejection by framing:
  - a sketch labelled "true for everyone" is rated only average to good
  - an astrology label is rated lower than a "scientific test" label, and some students dismissed accuracy as mere generality
  - no birth data lowers ratings

  — [Hyman 1977](https://www.skepdic.com/Hyman_cold_reading.htm)
- Self-discrepant feedback is judged less accurate (2021 credibility account). — [SAGE 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976211049439)
- Self-serving bias can cancel the Barnum effect for unflattering statements. — [SBP Journal](https://aws.sbp-journal.com/index.php/sbp/article/view/1190)

### Inferences
Likely triggers of "not me", each tied to the findings above:
1. A firm, *specific* claim that contradicts the user's self-view. This is "inverted feedback."
2. An unmistakable platitude that is obviously true of everyone. This is "trivial feedback," and it lowers uniqueness ratings.
3. Two bald contradictory claims with no frame such as "depending on…" or "on the surface vs inside."
4. Life-situation claims that don't fit ("your relationship," for a single user; "your boss," for a student). These turn hits into obvious misses.
5. Heavy or global negativity.
6. Text that announces itself as generic ("everyone feels this today").

Repetition across days is a likely added trigger in an app. If users compare texts or see the same line again, the illusion of uniqueness collapses. This is inferred from the "for everyone" framing finding.

### Gaps
- No direct study was found of daily horoscope apps, or of how repetition and cross-user comparison affect acceptance.
- No quantitative data on internal contradiction within a single reading as a rejection cause. It is inferred from the inverted-feedback and Rainbow Ruse logic.

## 7. Personalised feedback in apps: tailored vs targeted vs generic (health communication)

### Takeaway
Tailoring built on individual assessment reliably beats generic messages, but the effect is small (r ≈ .07). It works because personally relevant messages get more attention and deeper processing. A generic message that happens to fit a person works almost as well, and personalisation by name alone is the weakest form.

### Cited Findings
- Noar, Benac & Harris (2007). "Does tailoring matter? Meta-analytic review of tailored print health behavior change interventions." *Psychological Bulletin*, 133(4), 673–693. 57 studies, cumulative N = 58,454. The weighted mean effect of tailoring is r = .074.
  - Significant moderators: comparison condition, behaviour, population, type of print material, number of contacts, follow-up length, theoretical concepts tailored on, and whether demographics or behaviour were tailored on.

  — [PubMed 17592961](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17592961/)
- Kreuter & Wray (2003). "Tailored and targeted health communication: strategies for enhancing information relevance." *Am. J. Health Behavior*, 27 Suppl 3.
  - Tailored messages stimulate more cognitive activity than non-tailored ones.
  - A non-tailored message that happens to fit a given person has similar effects.
  - Tailoring is proven, but not the only route to relevance.

  — [PubMed 14672383](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14672383/)
- Definitions, as used in the literature:

  | Term | What it means |
  |---|---|
  | Tailored | assessment-based, individually focused |
  | Personalised | e.g. only the recipient's name inserted into generic text |
  | Targeted | aimed at a population segment |
  | Generic | one message for all |

  The mechanism is the elaboration likelihood model: personally applicable messages are processed more actively. — [PMC citing paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2078327); [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/tailored-and-targeted-health-communication-strategies-for-enhanci/)
- Later computer-tailoring meta-analyses build on Noar et al. and describe the mean effect as "relatively small." — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3171505)

### Inferences
- Inserting the name or sign ("personalised") is the weakest layer. Text that branches on *assessed* user attributes ("tailored") is stronger, especially self-reported traits, life situation and current concerns. Tailoring on what users *say about themselves* fits both Kreuter's tailoring and Swann's self-verification.
- Noar's moderators include "theoretical concepts tailored on." Tailoring on psychologically meaningful variables (goals, concerns) is likely to beat tailoring on demographics alone.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis was found on personalised feedback *pages* in consumer personality or astrology apps (for example 16Personalities-style result pages).
- Effect sizes for "personalised by name only" vs fully tailored were not retrieved.

### Writing rules derived from the evidence (for deterministic daily-reading text)

These rules are inferences that synthesise the findings above. Each names its supporting evidence.

1. **Show the personal data being used.** Reference the user's full birth date, sign, and today's Moon or numerology explicitly. Acceptance rose from about 3.2 to 4.98 as birth data went from none to full date (Snyder, via Hyman).
2. **Never say or imply "for everyone."** Frame text as computed for this user on this day. "For you" labelling raises ratings by about 0.5–1.5 points (Snyder & Shenkel). Avoid phrases like "many people today…".
3. **Aim for roughly 75/25 positive/negative.** Positive lines should sound specific. Negative lines should stay general, mild and occasional ("at times…") (Hyman item studies; Dickson & Kelly favourability).
4. **Write negatives as inner, temporary and redeemable.** Use forms like "a part of you that others don't see," "lately," "you may catch yourself…". Pair each with a strength or a way through (Forer sketch structure; Pollyanna Pearl).
5. **Use inner-vs-outer contrasts.** "Calm on the outside, more is going on inside" is the most reusable Barnum pattern (Forer).
6. **Use Rainbow Ruse only with an explicit bridge.** Write "you can be both X and Y, depending on who you're with" or "today the Y side may come out." Don't state bare opposites, which reads as inverted or contradictory feedback.
7. **Leave open slots the reader fills.** "A conversation you've been postponing," "someone whose message you've been waiting for." The user supplies the specifics (Fuzzy Fact; Hyman's subjective validation).
8. **Avoid hard, falsifiable specifics about life situation.** No "your boss," "your partner" or "your children" unless the user told the app. Use conditional or neutral nouns ("someone close," "a work or study matter"). Inverted and mismatched feedback is detected.
9. **Avoid obvious platitudes.** "Life has ups and downs" is trivial feedback and lowers perceived uniqueness. Each line needs a concrete-sounding hook such as a time of day, an action or a sensory detail.
10. **Keep one reading internally consistent.** Tone, score and category texts must not contradict each other. If the score is low and the love text is glowing, reconcile them explicitly ("overall a slower day, but…").
11. **Ask, don't infer.** A short self-description at onboarding (traits, current focus or concern, relationship/work status) lets text branch on what the user believes about themselves. This beats demographic inference (Swann self-verification; Kreuter tailoring; Noar moderators).
12. **Affirm self-declared strengths and gently acknowledge self-declared weaknesses.** Users accept negative content that matches their own self-view and find it more credible (Swann; 2021 credibility account).
13. **Make the first sentence warm and "on target."** Early context colours how later statements are read (Hyman priming; ELIZA and Garfinkel effects).
14. **Keep the future open and gently optimistic.** "Things can improve" and unverifiable-but-hopeful predictions land, and misses are forgotten (Pollyanna Pearl; Rowland). Avoid confident, date-specific promises that can visibly fail.
15. **Use calendar and seasonal hooks.** Weekdays, month-end, holidays and seasons raise apparent specificity at no risk (SMU "seasonal guess").
16. **Use life-stage statements keyed to age.** Age from the birth date allows age-appropriate themes, such as reassessment around 40 or direction-seeking in the early 20s (Jacques Statement; definition partly unverified).
17. **Lend authority to the method, not to the voice.** Show the "how" (sky, element, number) confidently but modestly. Prestige of the procedure raises acceptance, and grand claims invite challenge (Dickson & Kelly; Hyman rules 1 and 3).
18. **Avoid visible repetition.** Deterministic variety across days, and across users who compare readings, protects the "illusion of uniqueness." Identical lines spotted twice turn the reading into "for everyone" (inferred from the framing findings).
19. **Do not over-rely on a flattering lie when the truth is known.** Real fit adds acceptance on top of the Barnum effect (Poškus 2014; Wyman & Vyse 2008). Where the app knows something real (user inputs, streaks, past choices), use it.
